My Career Journey

From tutoring to analytics, with a lot of learning along the way

Where I Am Now

I'm currently a Data and Analytics Analyst at Fisher Investments in Plano, Texas, where I've been since December 2021. I started there as an Associate, moved into a Specialist role, and now work as an Analyst. It has been a good place to grow because the work keeps asking for both technical skill and clear communication.

My day-to-day work involves digging into firm-wide data with SQL, R, and Python to support business decisions. I spend a lot of time building dashboards and reporting tools in Power BI and Excel so teams can track what matters without having to piece it together themselves.

One of my favorite parts of the job is the mix of exploration and cleanup. Sometimes that means finding patterns in client segments or reporting behavior, and sometimes it means automating work that used to eat up hours every week. I like projects that make the data better and make other people's jobs easier at the same time.

Starting Out at BDO

Before Fisher, I spent about two and a half years at BDO USA in their Industry Specialty Services practice out of McLean, Virginia. That job showed me how different analytics work can look from one industry to the next.

I worked with clients in finance, healthcare, and federal contracting. Different industries, same basic problem: large messy datasets that needed to become something usable. A lot of the work was about turning disconnected sources into analysis people could actually act on.

Some of the most memorable projects involved fraud detection and contract compliance models. I also built dashboards in Power BI and Qlik Sense, which taught me a lot about presenting analytical work to both technical and non-technical audiences. That ability to translate the work clearly is still one of the most useful skills I carry with me.

Where It All Started

Looking back, a lot of this started when I worked as a math and physics tutor at Texas A&M University-Texarkana from 2017 to 2019. I was finishing my bachelor's degree then and tutoring gave me repeated practice in breaking down difficult ideas until they actually made sense to somebody else.

I worked with students one-on-one and in groups, helped with lecture prep, and ran review sessions. It was good training for the kind of work I still enjoy now: taking something technical, organizing it, and making it easier to understand.

Never Stop Learning

Even while working full-time, I've kept going back to school. I'm currently pursuing an M.S. in Artificial Intelligence at the University of Texas and an M.S. in Mathematics with a Data Science concentration at East Texas A&M University. It is a lot, but I like the balance of classroom theory and day-to-day analytical work.

The AI program has helped me get deeper into machine learning methods, while the math program keeps sharpening the foundations that make analytical work hold together. Each one makes the other more useful.

Coursework has also turned into some projects I'm genuinely proud of, including health analytics work with logistic regression and ensemble methods, an NBA salary prediction project, and log analysis work using PySpark. Those projects have been useful because they let me practice new ideas on problems that are concrete enough to be interesting.

The Tools I Use

Python and R are still my main tools for analysis and modeling, and I work comfortably with SQL across several flavors. For dashboards and reporting, I've spent a lot of time with Power BI, Qlik Sense, and Excel. For machine learning work, I lean on the usual toolkit of regression, classification, clustering, and dimensionality reduction depending on the problem.

I've also worked with Spark, Hadoop, AWS, and Jupyter-based workflows, but the more important lesson has been knowing when not to overcomplicate things. Sometimes Excel is enough. Sometimes you need a proper pipeline. Choosing the right level of complexity matters more than reaching for the fanciest tool.

What's Next

What I like most about this field is that there is always something new to learn, but the core work stays familiar: ask a better question, make the data understandable, and build something useful from it.

I'm always happy to talk with people about analytics, math, sports projects, or work that's trying to bridge technical depth with plain-language communication.
