Introduction and Mathematical Foundations

An overview of deep learning, statistical concepts, linear algebra, and gradient computation

Introduction and Statistical Preliminaries

What is Deep Learning?

Deep Learning in Everyday Life

Deep learning has become ubiquitous in modern technology, often operating behind the scenes without users realizing it. Photo management systems use deep learning algorithms to automatically categorize images, recognize faces, and suggest tags for friends and family members. Modern smartphones leverage deep learning for facial recognition to unlock devices, with algorithms that can distinguish between the device owner and others with high accuracy.

Natural language processing applications, from chatbots to voice assistants like Siri, Alexa, and Google Assistant, increasingly rely on deep learning models at their core. These systems can understand context, generate human-like responses, and adapt to individual speaking patterns. The lecturer emphasizes that these technologies are fundamentally "powered by deep learning" at their core, representing a significant shift from earlier rule-based or simple machine learning approaches.

Definition and Characteristics

At its essence, deep learning is machine learning that works exceptionally well. The key distinguishing feature is scalability—deep learning models can effectively leverage vast amounts of data. Unlike traditional machine learning methods that may plateau in performance, deep learning models often continue to improve as more data becomes available.

A crucial advantage of deep learning is its ability to learn representations that generalize to new concepts. Rather than requiring manual feature engineering, deep neural networks can automatically discover hierarchical feature representations from raw data. For example, in image recognition, early layers might learn to detect edges, middle layers combine these into shapes, and deeper layers recognize objects or scenes. This representational learning capability represents a significant advancement over traditional machine learning methods.

Relationship Between Statistics, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning

Understanding the historical relationship between these fields provides important context. Machine learning originated from statistics and was once treated as a subfield of statistics. However, as machine learning evolved to emphasize prediction over explanation, it became increasingly distinct from classical statistics, which focuses more on inference and hypothesis testing.

Today, machine learning and statistics are somewhat disjoint fields, though they maintain significant overlap. Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning that continues to overlap with statistics, particularly in areas like probabilistic modeling and Bayesian methods. The fundamental connection across all three fields is their shared use of models—specifically statistical models—to predict or measure properties of data.

Models Across Fields

The purpose and nature of models differ across these fields:

  • Statistics: Models are used to analyze and explain data, understand trends, and make inferences about populations from samples.
  • Machine Learning: Models focus on making predictions on unseen data, with less emphasis on interpretability and more on predictive accuracy.
  • Deep Learning: Uses a specific class of models called deep networks (or neural networks). These models are characterized by being "much bigger" and capable of fitting data "much, much better" than traditional models, often containing millions or even billions of parameters.
Example: Weather Data and Statistical Modeling

Consider raw weather data consisting of many temperature measurements over time—perhaps hourly or even per-minute measurements collected over years. Statistics can summarize this data through various transformations and aggregations:

  • Maximum and minimum temperature per day
  • Monthly averages
  • Multi-year averages and trends

However, some questions—such as whether winters are getting warmer—may be difficult to visualize directly and require statistical models. A statistical model is a function that maps input data \( x \) to an output. For example, \( x \) could represent the date of measurement, and the output could be the predicted temperature.

A key feature of statistical models is that they are parametric, meaning they depend on parameters denoted \( \theta \). These parameters allow the model to adjust and fit to the data. For instance, in a linear model \( y = \theta_1 x + \theta_0 \), the parameters \( \theta_0 \) and \( \theta_1 \) control the intercept and slope, respectively.

Historical Sketch of Deep Learning

The roots of deep learning trace back to the perceptron, introduced in a paper around 1957 (the lecturer mentions some uncertainty about exact dates). Early researchers were highly optimistic, believing that machine learning would solve almost any problem within about 10 years. However, this enthusiasm was short-lived due to significant constraints:

  • Limited data: Unlike today's era of big data, datasets were small and often manually curated
  • Extremely limited compute: Modern GPUs didn't exist, and computers were orders of magnitude slower
  • Small models: Given the constraints, models couldn't be very large or complex

As these limitations became clear, funding decreased dramatically. A influential book on perceptrons attempted to discredit early perceptron-based methods, highlighting fundamental limitations. The result was an "AI winter" lasting almost 20 years, during which neural network research was largely abandoned.

The revival began when backpropagation was "discovered for the final time" (the lecturer's phrasing acknowledges multiple independent discoveries throughout history). This algorithm enabled training of bigger and more interesting neural networks by efficiently computing gradients through the network. Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, classical machine learning methods remained ahead of neural networks in most applications.

A pivotal moment came around 2005, when a research group rebranded "neural networks" as "deep learning" (the lecturer claims the term didn't exist before this). This rebranding was partly necessitated by the field's reputation—including neural networks in a paper title at top machine learning venues reportedly increased chances of rejection. The new name helped the field gain renewed acceptance and momentum.

Since then, deep learning has achieved state-of-the-art performance across many domains:

  • By 2010: State-of-the-art in speech recognition
  • By 2012: State-of-the-art in image recognition (computer vision), marked by the breakthrough ImageNet performance of AlexNet
  • By 2014: Almost the entire computer vision field had adopted deep networks
  • By 2017: Deep learning took over natural language processing with the transformer architecture

Today, there is virtually no machine learning field where deep learning is not at least a competitor, and in many domains it has become the dominant approach.

Summary

Deep learning is a subfield of machine learning that studies deep networks—powerful, generalizable models capable of fitting immense amounts of data. These networks can automatically learn hierarchical representations from data, enabling breakthroughs across speech recognition, computer vision, natural language processing, and many other domains.

Statistical Notation and Concepts for Deep Learning

Motivation: Why Statistics Matters

Understanding statistics is crucial for deep learning because machine learning was born out of statistics and inherited much of its notation (though with some modifications). The lecturer notes that there tends to be more "abuse of notation" in machine learning and deep learning than in statistics, making familiarity with the original statistical concepts important. This section serves as a notation review rather than a complete statistics guide.

Randomness and Probability

Consider a simple example: tossing a coin. We don't know the outcome (heads or tails) in advance, making it a random process. From a physics perspective, one might argue that if we could perfectly observe all conditions—the exact weight distribution of the coin, where the hand applies force, the exact force magnitude, air resistance, and landing surface—we could predict the outcome. However, in practice, we cannot observe all these factors perfectly, so the process appears random to us.

If a coin is unbiased or fair, there is roughly a 50/50 chance of heads versus tails. Interestingly, the lecturer notes that coin tosses are not exactly 50/50—the initially-top side has a slightly higher chance of landing on top again due to the physics of coin rotation. This has been verified through large-scale experiments and detailed physics analysis.

Basic Probability Notation

We use the following notation:

  • Random variable: Denoted \( x \)
  • Specific value (outcome): Denoted \( a \)
  • Event: "\( x \) taking value \( a \)"
  • Probability: Written \( p(x) \) or more precisely \( p(x = a) \)

In practice, the notation \( p(x) \) is often used when the context makes the specific value clear, though \( p(x = a) \) is more precise.

Discrete vs. Continuous Outcomes

Outcomes can be either discrete or continuous:

  • Discrete example: A coin toss with outcomes heads or tails (categorical outcomes)
  • Continuous example: The angle \( \alpha \) at which a coin lands on the table

For continuous random variables, the probability of landing at exactly a specific angle \( \alpha \) is effectively zero (or technically undefined). Instead, we work with cumulative distribution concepts: the probability of landing between \( 40^\circ \) and \( 50^\circ \) is meaningful.

For continuous variables, we use probability density rather than probability. The probability density is defined as:

\[ \text{Probability density} = \frac{\text{Probability of landing in a range}}{\text{Length of the range}}. \]

Unified Notation Convention

In this course, a unified notation convention will be used that does not distinguish between:

  • Probability mass (for discrete variables)
  • Probability density (for continuous variables)

The symbol \( p(x) \) will be used for both. The variable \( x \) may be discrete or continuous; the course will "not care about which one it is" (lecturer phrasing), focusing on the underlying mathematical principles rather than technical distinctions.

Basic Properties of Probabilities and Densities

All probability distributions (whether discrete or continuous) satisfy:

  • Non-negativity: \[ p(x) \ge 0. \]
  • Normalization:
    • Discrete case: \( \sum_x p(x) = 1 \)
    • Continuous case: \( \int p(x)\,dx = 1 \)

The function \( p \) maps:

  • Discrete: Outcomes to values in \( [0,1] \)
  • Continuous: Real values to non-negative real values (note: densities can exceed 1, unlike discrete probabilities)

Distributions as a Recurring Tool

Probability distributions will be used throughout the course to:

  • Describe data and data-generating processes
  • Describe outputs of models
  • Reason about labels attached to data

For example, the visual world you observe daily can be viewed as a probability distribution over visual stimuli. Common sights (like faces, cars, or trees) correspond to high-probability events, while novel or unusual sights correspond to low-probability events. Similarly, weather and temperature over time can be viewed as distributions over possible temperature values.

Three Categories of Distributions

It's important to distinguish between three types of distributions:

  • Data-generating distribution: The real-world process that produces data (e.g., the climate/weather system itself)
  • Empirical distribution: What we actually observe as samples (e.g., measured weather data from sensors)
  • Model distribution: The distribution implied by a model fit to the empirical data

In general, these three distributions are not the same, though in simple examples they may coincide. The goal of machine learning is often to make the model distribution match the data-generating distribution as closely as possible, using the empirical distribution as our only window into the true data-generating process.

Expectation

The expectation (or expected value) of a function \( f(x) \) under distribution \( p \) is a weighted average:

  • Discrete case: Weighted sum over outcomes \[ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x p(x) f(x) \]
  • Continuous case: Integral instead of sum \[ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \int p(x) f(x) dx \]

The same expectation symbol is used for both; interpretation depends on whether \( x \) is discrete or continuous. Notational shortcuts are common:

  • Drop the \( x \) subscript if implied: \( \mathbb{E}[f] \)
  • Drop the explicit distribution \( p \) if clear from context
  • Drop function arguments if clear: \( \mathbb{E}[f] \) instead of \( \mathbb{E}[f(x)] \)

Expectation has useful linearity properties: \[ \mathbb{E}[f + g] = \mathbb{E}[f] + \mathbb{E}[g], \qquad \mathbb{E}[c f] = c \mathbb{E}[f], \] where \( c \) is a constant.

Mean and Variance

The mean (or expected value) of a distribution is the average value it takes, representing the "center" of the distribution. For example, in a Gaussian (normal) distribution, the mean is the peak of the bell curve.

The variance measures the spread or deviation of values around the mean. A distribution with small variance has values clustered tightly around the mean, while large variance indicates values are spread out.

Important caveats:

  • Discrete probabilities: Satisfy \( p(x) \le 1 \)
  • Continuous densities: Can exceed 1 (a common coding pitfall if one assumes otherwise)
  • Variance: May be finite for discrete distributions, but continuous distributions can have infinite variance

Sampling

Sampling means drawing outcomes from a distribution. Examples include:

  • Rolling a fair die (sampling from a uniform discrete distribution)
  • Typing on a keyboard (sometimes hitting the wrong key—sampling from a distribution of possible keystrokes)
  • Taking a photo (sampling a snapshot from the visual world distribution)

Important point: Samples are always biased in the sense that a finite number of samples will not exactly match the true distribution. Exact matching occurs only in the limit of infinitely many samples. For example, with 10 coin flips, getting exactly 5 heads and 5 tails has a relatively small probability, and repeating the experiment yields wide variation. This sampling variability is fundamental to understanding why we need statistical methods.

Statistical Models

A statistical model is a function with parameters \( \theta \) that maps inputs to outputs. Two main types:

  • Regression: Maps real-valued inputs to real-valued outputs
    • Example: Linear regression fits a line to data points
    • Output: A continuous value (e.g., predicted temperature given a date)
  • Classification: Maps inputs to class outputs or probabilities over classes
    • Maps real values to probabilities over discrete events
    • Probabilities are positive and sum to one
    • Example: Image classification (dog vs. cat vs. bird)

In summary, models typically map "real values to real values" (regression) or "real values to probabilities" (classification).

Learning from Data

In machine learning, the goal is to find parameters \( \theta \) given data. Data can be viewed as samples from a data-generating distribution. Two kinds of data:

  • Unlabeled data: Raw data without target values (e.g., internet text/images, raw photo collections)
  • Labeled data: Data with labels attached (e.g., photos with tags, dates with corresponding temperatures)

This course will focus primarily on labeled data. The goal is to learn a model that, on new data, can infer the labels that the new data should have.

Summary of Statistics Review

Key takeaways:

  • A statistical model learns to predict \( y \) from input \( x \) using parameters \( \theta \)
  • Expectations are weighted averages under \( p(x) \)
  • Mean and variance measure the center and spread of a distribution
  • Distributions are fundamental tools for describing data, models, and learning processes

Linear Algebra Basics for Deep Learning

Purpose of Linear Algebra

Linear algebra serves as the mathematical language of deep learning. It enables compact notation and efficient computation for large-scale operations. The emphasis in this course is on notation and computation rather than geometric interpretations, though geometric intuition can be valuable for understanding.

Vectors

A vector is a 1-dimensional array of numbers (analogous to a Python list of numbers). Notation: bold lowercase letter, e.g., \( \mathbf{v} \). Elements are denoted \( \mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n) \), where \( n \) is the dimension of the vector.

Elementwise Operations

For two vectors of the same size (e.g., \( \mathbf{v} \) and \( \mathbf{w} \)):

  • Addition and subtraction are performed elementwise
  • Multiplication and division can also be performed elementwise

Notation convention:

  • Elementwise multiplication is denoted by a dot: \( \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{w} \)
  • Matrix-vector or matrix-matrix multiplication is written without a dot: \( A\mathbf{v} \)
  • Division refers to elementwise division

Matrices

A matrix is a 2-dimensional array of numbers. Entries are identified by their row and column position. Notation: typically uppercase letters, e.g., \( A \), with entry \( A_{ij} \) denoting the element in row \( i \) and column \( j \).

Transpose

Transposing a matrix mirrors it along its diagonal, swapping position \( (i,j) \) with position \( (j,i) \). The transpose of matrix \( A \) is denoted \( A^\top \).

Transposing a vector converts a column vector into a row vector (and vice versa). This becomes important for gradient notation because gradients are often written as row vectors, while inputs are column vectors.

Matrix Multiplication

Given two matrices \( A \) and \( B \), matrix multiplication produces an output matrix \( C = AB \).

Dimension compatibility: The number of columns in \( A \) must equal the number of rows in \( B \).

Computation: For each entry \( C_{ij} \) in the result:

  1. Take row \( i \) of \( A \) and column \( j \) of \( B \)
  2. Multiply corresponding elements elementwise
  3. Sum the products to get \( C_{ij} \)

Example: If \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 4} \) and \( B \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 3} \), then \( C = AB \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 3} \). The "4" must match between the columns of \( A \) and rows of \( B \); the resulting dimensions are 2×3.

Matrix-Vector Multiplication

Vectors are column vectors by default. If \( M \) is a matrix and \( \mathbf{v} \) is a column vector, then \( M\mathbf{v} \) produces another column vector.

For row vectors, the vector must be multiplied on the left using the transpose: \( \mathbf{v}^\top M \).

Dot Product (Inner Product)

The dot product (or inner product) of two vectors is a scalar:

\[ \mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i} v_i w_i \]

This is computed by multiplying corresponding elements and summing. The result is a scalar (sometimes described as a "1×1 matrix").

The outer product \( \mathbf{v} \mathbf{w}^\top \) produces a matrix, where the \( (i,j) \) entry is \( v_i w_j \).

Norms

The Euclidean norm (or \( L_2 \) norm) of a vector measures its "length" in space:

\[ \|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2} \]

This is computed by squaring each element, summing them, then taking the square root. While this has a geometric interpretation (distance from origin), the course focuses on computation.

The matrix norm (Frobenius norm) is the sum of squares of all matrix elements:

\[ \|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} A_{ij}^2} \]

Example: Centering Data

Problem: Given values \( a_1, \dots, a_n \), compute \( \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} - \mu \), where \( \mu \) is the mean of all \( a_i \).

Raw Python approach:

  1. Loop to sum entries
  2. Divide by length to compute mean
  3. Loop again to subtract mean from each entry

Linear algebra expression: \[ \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} - \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{1}}{n}, \] where \( \mathbf{1} \) is a vector of ones, so \( \mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{1} \) gives the sum of all \( a_i \).

PyTorch expression: \[ \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a} - \text{mean}(\mathbf{a}), \] where mean computes the average of the values. This compact notation is one reason why linear algebra frameworks are so powerful for deep learning.

Tensors

Motivation and Definition

Vectors (1D) and matrices (2D) are not sufficient for representing all types of data in deep learning. Tensors extend linear algebra to represent higher-dimensional data. A tensor is an \( n \)-dimensional array (ndarray) of numbers.

Key attributes:

  • Shape: The size along each dimension (e.g., \( (3, 224, 224) \) for an image)
  • Number of dimensions: The length of the shape tuple (also called rank or order)

Examples of Tensor Dimensionality

  • 1D tensor: Vector (e.g., \( [1, 2, 3] \))
  • 2D tensor: Matrix (e.g., \( [[1, 2], [3, 4]] \))
  • 3D tensor:
    • Image (height × width × channels)
    • Spectrogram of a waveform (time × frequency × channels)
  • 4D tensor:
    • Video (height × width × channels × time)
    • Batch of images (batch_size × height × width × channels)

Image and Video Dimensions

An image is typically represented as a 3D tensor with dimensions:

  • Height: Number of pixels vertically
  • Width: Number of pixels horizontally
  • Channels: Number of color channels
    • RGB images: 3 channels (red, green, blue)
    • Hyperspectral images: 20–30 channels (as mentioned by the lecturer)
    • Grayscale: 1 channel

Video adds an additional time dimension, creating a 4D tensor.

PyTorch-Specific Notes

In PyTorch, tensors are the fundamental building block for representing values and data. Tensors have:

  • Shape: Dimension sizes and number of dimensions
  • dtype: Data type (e.g., integer, floating point) with selectable precision

The number of dimensions is given by the length of the shape tuple. For example, tensor.shape = (3, 224, 224) indicates a 3D tensor.

Gradients, Jacobians, and the Chain Rule for Deep Networks

Motivation: Optimization via Gradients

A fundamental question in deep learning is: how do we optimize (train) deep networks? One key insight is that a deep network is a gigantic nested function—functions inside functions inside functions. Training deep networks centers on computing partial derivatives and gradients of these nested functions, which is where the chain rule becomes essential.

Gradients Depend on Input/Output Dimensions

The dimensionality of gradients depends on the input and output dimensions of the function:

Case 1: Scalar → Scalar

For a function \( f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \), the derivative is a scalar. This is the familiar calculus derivative: \( \frac{df}{dx} \).

Case 2: Vector → Scalar

For a function \( f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R} \), the gradient with respect to the input vector \( \mathbf{x} \) is a vector: \[ \nabla_\mathbf{x} f = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \right)^\top \]

Note: Gradients are usually row vectors (lecturer emphasis), but this is written as a column vector here for clarity. The important point is that the gradient has the same dimensionality as the input.

Case 3: Vector → Vector

For a function \( f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \), the derivative object is a matrix with one partial derivative per (output, input) pair. This matrix is called the Jacobian: \[ J_f = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\ \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n} \end{bmatrix} \]

The Jacobian has shape \( m \times n \):

  • Rows correspond to output dimensions
  • Columns correspond to input dimensions

Chain Rule for Nested Functions

Consider two functions:

  • \( g_1: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \) (maps \( n \) values to \( m \) values)
  • \( g_2: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^k \) (maps \( m \) values to \( k \) values)

The composition \( h(x) = g_2(g_1(x)) \) maps \( \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^k \). To compute the Jacobian of \( h \), we use the chain rule:

\[ J_h = J_{g_2} \cdot J_{g_1} \]

More precisely:

  1. Compute the Jacobian of the inner function \( g_1 \) (has shape \( m \times n \))
  2. Compute the Jacobian of the outer function \( g_2 \) evaluated at \( g_1(x) \) (has shape \( k \times m \))
  3. Multiply the Jacobians (matrix multiplication) to get \( J_h \) with shape \( k \times n \)

This is the fundamental principle that makes training deep networks possible.

Backpropagation

Backpropagation is "essentially the chain rule" computed in a very specific way. Instead of computing full Jacobians (which can be extremely large), backpropagation computes gradients efficiently by working backward through the network, reusing intermediate computations. The details of how backpropagation is implemented efficiently will be covered in later modules.

Key Takeaways

  • Gradients are usually row vectors (lecturer emphasis)
  • The chain rule is the core component enabling gradients through complex nested functions
  • Training deep networks amounts to computing the chain rule over large Jacobians efficiently
  • Backpropagation provides an efficient implementation of the chain rule for neural networks
